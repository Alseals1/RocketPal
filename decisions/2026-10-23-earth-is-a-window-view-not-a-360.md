# Earth is a window view of one real photo, not a 360 degree picture

**Decided by:** the user chose "a real hover view from the International Space Station"; the main agent chose how to build it.

## What
Landing at Earth shows NASA astronaut photo **iss073e0247726** (an aurora over a cloudy ocean, Earth's glowing edge, stars, and part of the station's solar array) seen through a **drawn window frame**, with a small look-around. Same dots, fact cards, Listen and Orbit as Mars. The photo is placed on a flat plane at exactly the angles the lens saw (73.7 x 53.1 degrees), so it looks like the photo and not a bent sphere. On a screen wider than the photo the camera zooms in so the photo fills the width (no black bars); on a tall phone you can look a bit sideways.

## Why not a real 360 degree picture
NASA offers no 360 degree still from the station's Cupola. The only 360 degree Cupola material found was from Roscosmos (a video) and ESA (a panoramic tour), which are different licensors with different terms, so they were not used (and a licensing question goes to the user first). Faking a sphere from one photo would have claimed more than we have.

## Alternatives considered
- Stretching the photo round a sphere (wrong geometry, and it would pretend to be a 360 degree view).
- A plain card with no picture (loses the point of the feature).
- Other photos from NASA's astronaut photography archive (one wide aurora photo was the most teachable: ocean, clouds, the air's edge, stars, and the station itself).

## Licensing
NASA JSC's astronaut-photography terms say reuse is welcome where NASA holds no copyright, and none is noted for this photo. That matches the user's standing decision of 2026-10-23 ("Yes, use the NASA photos"), so the user was not asked again; this note is here so the user can overrule it. Nobody has contacted NASA. Details are in `CREDITS.md`.

The credit line, the photo number, and "The window frame is drawn."
